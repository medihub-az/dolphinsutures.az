Surgical sutures and meshes

The choice of material, needle geometry, and size must be tailored to each tissue type. Practical selection tables, absorption timelines, and a comparison of hernia meshes are the working tools for getting there.

This section is technical-educational material for medical professionals — the specific clinical decision always rests with the surgeon.

  • Absorbable for temporary support: subcutaneous tissue, bowel, uterus
  • Non-absorbable for permanent support: vessels, fascia, orthopedics
  • Monofilament provokes less tissue reaction; lower risk of bacterial contamination
  • Braided provides better knot security

First decision: absorbable or non-absorbable

A suture should support the tissue only until healing is complete. If the tissue regains strength within 2–3 weeks (subcutaneous tissue, mucosa), an absorbable suture is sufficient. Fascia, vascular anastomoses, the sternum, and orthopedic fixation, by contrast, require permanent or long-term support — in these cases non-absorbable sutures are preferred.

Second decision: monofilament or braided

A monofilament suture glides through tissue; compared with braided suture it carries a lower risk of bacterial contamination and provokes less tissue reaction — so in potentially contaminated fields and in skin it is preferred over braided sutures. A braided suture, in turn, offers easier handling and a more secure knot, which is why it is widely used for ligatures and in deep layers. Antibacterial (triclosan) coated variants compensate for the braided suture's drawback.

Third decision: size (USP)

The USP (United States Pharmacopeia) suture system is a standardized numerical scale used to define the diameter and gauge of surgical sutures. Created to apply uniform sizing across different materials and manufacturers, this system ranks sutures from the thickest to the finest. In the USP system, the larger the number, the thinner the suture: 2-0 is thick, while 6-0 is very fine. The rule: choose the finest suture that meets the requirement. A suture that is too thick cuts through tissue and provokes excessive foreign-body reaction; one that is too fine may break.

Tissue / procedure Recommended suture Typical USP
Skin (interrupted closure) Monofilament non-absorbable (Linex, Duracare) 3-0 – 5-0
Subcutaneous / intradermal Monofilament absorbable (Petcryl Mono) 3-0 – 5-0
Fascia, aponeurosis Long-term absorbable (Duracryl) or non-absorbable 0 – 2-0
Bowel, anastomosis Absorbable braided / monofilament 3-0 – 4-0
Vascular anastomosis Polypropylene monofilament (Duracare) 5-0 – 7-0
Gynecology, uterus Polyglactin 910 (Petcryl 910) 0 – 2-0

Fourth decision: closure technique — a matter no less important than material selection

In midline abdominal closure, choosing the right material is not enough; closure technique matters at least as much. The European Hernia Society (EHS) guideline on the closure of abdominal wall incisions rests on specific parameters: a continuous suture, a slowly absorbable monofilament, and a suture-length-to-wound-length ratio of at least 4:1.

The practical meaning of this ratio is the small bites technique: stitches placed about 5 mm from the edge and every 5 mm — as opposed to large bites (1 cm or more). The double-blind, multicenter randomized trial known as STITCH compared exactly these two techniques and showed a significantly lower one-year incisional hernia rate in the small-bites group.

A practical summary for selection: in fascia, not a few wide stitches with a thick suture, but many small stitches with a fine suture — and a slowly absorbable monofilament that provides long-term support, such as Duracryl (polydioxanone).

  • Cutting suits tough tissues — skin, fascia
  • Reverse cutting — prevents the needle hole from enlarging
  • 1/2 and 3/8 are the most commonly used curvatures

The needle point is chosen according to the tissue being sutured

  • Taper point — the needle's round body tapers to a sharp tip. It separates tissue without cutting, and the hole closes around the suture after the needle passes. Used for vessels, bowel, fascia, muscle, and subcutaneous tissue.
  • Conventional cutting — the cutting edge is on the inner (concave) side of the needle. Suitable for tough tissues such as skin and dense connective tissue.
  • Reverse cutting — the cutting edge is on the outer (convex) side of the needle. It prevents the hole from enlarging as the suture is pulled through; considered the preferred choice for skin, oral mucosa, and aesthetic surgery.
  • Blunt point — for parenchymal organs and situations with a high risk of needlestick injury.
  • Spatula point — ophthalmology: passage in the plane between thin tissue layers.

Needle curvature depends on the depth of the suture

Curvature is expressed as a fraction of a circle. The greater the curvature, the less space is needed to rotate the needle — so more curved needles suit deep, narrow spaces:
3/8 circle — convenient in superficial, open fields (skin).
1/2 circle — allows the needle to be rotated in narrow, deep spaces (pelvis, bowel).
1/4 circle — ophthalmology.
5/8 circle — very confined spaces (urology).
Straight needles are for superficial skin sutures.

A practical rule

Needle length should match the depth of the suture: a needle that is too long cannot be rotated in a confined field, while one that is too short will not reach the required depth. Suture size and needle size must also be matched — in our catalog every item lists needle type, length, and curvature, all selectable with filters.

The mesh material is not an aesthetic choice — it follows from the plane of placement. If the mesh is placed outside the peritoneum (extraperitoneal), a plain monofilament polypropylene mesh is sufficient: strong, inexpensive, and well integrated. But if the mesh will remain in contact with bowel inside the abdominal cavity, one side must carry an anti-adhesion barrier layer — otherwise the risk of bowel adhesion and fistula increases.

  • Knit 3D — a preformed anatomical contour: laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair.

  • Teflene — polypropylene + ePTFE barrier: intraperitoneal.
  • Ultralene — polyester + polyethylene layer: ventral hernia.

Antibacterial-coated sutures

The structure of a braided suture can create micro-harbors for bacteria. The triclosan coating targets exactly this problem: it limits bacterial colonization on and around the suture surface. In the Dolphin range, this coating is available in the Duracryl Plus (polydioxanone), Petcryl Plus (polyglactin 910), and Mono AB (poliglecaprone 25) lines.

An important note: an antibacterial suture does not replace sterility or surgical technique — it is an additional layer of protection and makes sense where the risk of infection is high.

Barbed sutures

The micro-barbs on the surface of a barbed suture anchor into tissue, so the suture does not need to be knotted. This yields three practical benefits: time (shorter operating time on long suture lines), even tension distribution (no concentration at a knot point), and the absence of knot material. In the Dolphin range: Durabarb and Durabarb Plus (polydioxanone, absorbable) and Polybarb (polypropylene, non-absorbable, loop design).

Typical applications: long continuous suture lines in abdominoplasty, mastopexy, gynecologic, and bariatric procedures.
